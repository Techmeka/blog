## The drawer you already know about

Somewhere in your house there is a drawer full of things that _almost_ fit. The vanity drawer of an IKEA Alex 5, loaded with eyeshadow palettes that lean against each other because the acrylic tray was built for a different drawer. The mechanic's tool chest where the three-eighths sockets live next to the half-inch sockets, except two of the half-inch ones went missing last Tuesday. The kitchen junk drawer where a roll of tape, a stapler, and seventeen rubber bands are all equally committed to the position they're in.

The drawer is a fixed shape, measured in millimetres by whoever built the furniture. The things inside are also fixed shapes, chosen by you over time. What goes between them — the organizer — is the only variable. And almost nothing on sale is built for your drawer **and** your things at the same time.

**There are three answers today.** All three are compromises.

- **Buy a stock-size tray on Amazon.** Pick the closest size. Live with the 23mm gap along one edge.
- **Download a Gridfinity bin and print it yourself.** Beautiful system — if you own a 3D printer, know your slicer, and have a weekend.
- **Find a custom maker on Etsy.** Message them, wait, discuss, quote, deposit, wait fourteen to twenty-one business days, hope the fit lands.

customOrg is a fourth option. Ten minutes in a browser, between fifteen and eighty Canadian dollars depending on the drawer, in your mailbox a week later, shipped from Toronto.

## How it works

Five handoffs. The first is yours.

1. **Design in a browser.** Open [design.customorg.com](https://design.customorg.com). Punch in width × depth × height in millimetres. Drag compartments onto a grid. If you're organizing tools, drop in silhouettes for your specific sockets, wrenches, ratchets. Pick a body colour (Jet Black, Slate, Bone, or Maple) and an accent. Rotate the 3D preview. First draft takes about ten minutes.

2. **Pay with a number that doesn't drift.** The price updates in real time as you edit, because the same code that would print your organizer is also computing its cost. Canada Post rates land live at checkout against the real parcel dimensions. What you see on the editor is what you pay — no handling-charge surprise.

3. **A human in Toronto approves the order.** Ten seconds of a human catching the "entered inches instead of millimetres" mistake before three hours of print time gets spent on it.

4. **Printed as one piece.** Industrial 3D printer, single piece, no assembly. Three to six hours on the floor depending on size. Drawers that exceed a single build plate split into pieces that fit together like a puzzle, each printed on its own plate.

5. **Shipped from Toronto.** Canada Post label, tracking email, typical arrival seven to ten business days. Longer on the prairie and maritime routes. One organizer, one parcel.

## Three things worth knowing

**The price moves as you drag.** No contact-us-for-a-quote, no deposit on an open-ended number. A vanity drawer, 258 × 414 millimetres, four centimetres deep — first draft is six compartments, the editor says $32. Make one compartment deeper for a taller brush: $41. Shave it back half a centimetre: $37. The editor is a conversation about trade-offs you were going to have anyway. And the price the browser shows is the price the server trusts — the browser doesn't send a number to checkout, it sends a design, and the server recomputes from scratch.

**Fit the drawer, not the SKU.** A Burlington mechanic with a Craftsman tool chest, middle drawer 482 × 398 millimetres — not a size anyone sells inserts for. He drops in silhouettes for his ten specific sockets, three ratchets, two torque wrenches, and a magnetic parts tray. The whole insert prints as one piece. Nothing rattles when the chest closes. If the fit is wrong, we reprint it at our cost — our measurements match what you entered, so if the fit's off, we made the mistake.

**Made in Toronto, shipped Canada-wide.** No cross-border customs. No "shipped from China in six to eight weeks." A 230 × 230 millimetre kitchen drawer insert ordered from Halifax on a Tuesday evening: printed Wednesday night, labelled Thursday morning, picked up by Canada Post on Thursday afternoon, in the mailbox the following Wednesday. Six business days, door to door, for an organizer that didn't exist the week before.
