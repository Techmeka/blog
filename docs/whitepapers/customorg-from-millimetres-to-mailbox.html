Part 2 · From browser to mailbox

Three steps, one afternoon, seven to ten business days.

A measurement goes in on one end; a finished organizer comes out the other. Three hand-offs in between. The first is yours. The other two are ours.

  • Design in your browser.

    Open design.customorg.com. Three numbers go in: the drawer's width, depth, and height, all in millimetres. Drag compartments onto the grid. Organizing tools? Drop in silhouettes for the specific sockets, wrenches, or ratchets you own. Pick a body colour (Jet Black, Slate, Bone, or Maple) and an accent. Rotate the 3D preview. First draft, about ten minutes.

    A design snapshot, rendered live in the editor as you drag.

  • Real-time pricing.

    The price updates as you edit. The same code that would print your organizer computes its cost from grams of plastic and minutes of machine time. Canada Post rates land live at checkout against your real parcel dimensions, so the shipping number is on screen before you confirm.

    What you see on the editor is what you pay. The server recomputes from your design at checkout; nothing drifts between the preview and the charge.

  • Approved and shipped from Toronto.

    An operator opens your order, confirms the layout makes sense, and marks it for print. Ten seconds of a human eye catches a measurement entered in inches, before three hours of print time gets spent on it. The organizer prints as a single piece on an industrial 3D printer (no assembly, no glue), then goes straight to Canada Post. Typical arrival: seven to ten business days across most of Canada.

Part 3 · Three things worth knowing

What makes customOrg different from the three answers above.

One

The price moves as you drag.

No quote form. No "contact us for pricing." No deposit on an open-ended number. The amount on the right-hand side of the editor updates in real time as you edit the geometry, because the same geometry that would print your organizer is also computing its cost: grams of plastic, print hours, a labour charge, a margin. Every input is defensible because every input is a real cost.

Scenario

A vanity drawer, 258 millimetres by 414 millimetres, four centimetres deep. First draft: six compartments, mostly shallow, one for brushes standing upright. The number starts at $32. The brush compartment grows taller (more plastic, more print time), and the number climbs to $41. The brush compartment shrinks half a centimetre. The number drops to $37. The editor is basically a conversation about trade-offs you were going to have anyway.

Two

Fit the drawer, not the SKU.

You measured the drawer. The organizer fits. That sounds trivial until you remember how often it hasn't been true. The industry has been training buyers for thirty years to pick the closest stock size and live with the gap. customOrg is retraining it, one drawer at a time, one insert at a time.

Scenario

A mechanic in Burlington with a Craftsman tool chest. The middle drawer is 482 millimetres by 398 millimetres. Not a size anyone sells inserts for. He drops in silhouettes for his ten specific sockets, three ratchets, two torque wrenches, and a magnetic parts tray, in a layout that fills the drawer. The whole insert prints as one piece. Nothing rattles when the chest closes.

If the fit is off, customOrg reprints it. Their measurements match what you entered. If something’s wrong, that’s on their side, not yours.

Three

Made in Toronto, shipped Canada-wide.

Every organizer is printed on a floor on the east side of Toronto. Every parcel is packed by a human. Every label is bought from Canada Post at live rates against the real parcel dimensions. No cross-border customs delay. No "shipped from China in six to eight weeks." No currency swing between the order and the arrival.

A 230 × 230 millimetre kitchen drawer insert ordered from a kitchen in Halifax on a Tuesday evening. Printed Wednesday night, labelled Thursday morning, picked up by Canada Post on Thursday afternoon, in the mailbox on the following Wednesday. Six business days, door to door, for an organizer that didn't exist the week before.

Part 4 · How customOrg got built

The build underneath the product.

The product story is a browser, 3D printers, and Canada Post. The build story is tasks, gates, and a stack that stayed small on purpose. Here’s how customOrg actually got made, and why the shape it has is the shape Techmeka wants every project to have.

The brief that opened the build was two lines long: a browser where the customer designs a drawer insert, and customOrg prints it to fit. Four months later the same stack ran live payments, live shipping, daily security scans, and an admin surface that only answers inside the Toronto tailnet. The trail between those two moments is what the rest of this section is about.

One

The work moved as tasks.

Any product of this scope — a browser editor, a pricing engine, a shipping integration, an admin surface, an identity layer — decomposes into smaller tasks. The visible work on customOrg ran the gamut: UI fit-and-finish, DNS and mail deliverability, third-party API edge cases, build-system races, test coverage, security hardening.

For most of them, AI did the decomposition itself. A high-level ask lands as a task; an AI session claims it, breaks it into the sub-tasks that actually need to ship, writes the plan comment, writes the code, writes the tests, and closes the loop with a summary. Humans review proposals, approve surfaces, and resolve ambiguity when the AI flags it. The Observers meter the work in flight: Tests Observer verifies, Shield Observer scans, State Observer pushes, Tasks Observer refuses closure without the full artifact set.

Every task had the same shape. A plan comment on claim. Running-log entries at every pivot. A provenance card from the automated test runner. A shield scan. The commit hashes that landed. A one-line closure summary with follow-up tasks linked out. By the time of the pre-launch audit in November, every line item on the audit sheet (sixty-four of them) had either closed through that workflow or been explicitly deferred with a documented reason. All sixty-four closed on the same day.

Two

The gates caught what reviewers wouldn’t.

Reviewers are good at style. Gates are what catch regressions. The Observer platform is eight products running as one governed pipe from plan to ship: every prompt budgeted, every action logged. This is what each one did during the customOrg build.

  • Tasks Observer. The Work OS where every task closed through a defined artifact set: plan, running-log, test provenance, commit hashes, closure summary. Tasks that tried to skip a step were blocked at the gate. Why the launch audit could read as a trail instead of a memory.
  • Cost Observer. The AI gateway. Every outbound LLM call attributed, cost-estimated, and budget-capped in flight. Per-project ceilings, multi-provider failover. The build never ran up an invisible bill.
  • Transmission Observer. The command center. Spawned and monitored the AI work sessions that moved the code, routed approval widgets to Tasks, threaded ninety-plus MCP tools between services. The reason the other seven feel like one platform instead of seven.
  • Tests Observer. The universal test runner. Ninety-four test files, roughly eight hundred and fifty tests at launch, every closed task carrying a provenance card the runner posted directly. Also captured client-side errors streaming in from the live site.
  • Automation Observer. Browser automation built for AI agents. Real Chromium, real WebKit, real iOS Safari driving the dev and live URLs; one API call returns screenshot, console, network, errors, and HTML in a single round-trip. Caught layout regressions no unit test would have.
  • Shield Observer. Twenty-seven scanners (AI-specific ones include prompt-injection scanning and live DLP), five hundred-plus controls mapped across OWASP, GDPR, PCI, SOC 2. Pre-commit hook on the dev side; daily DAST against the live VPS; external probe for the Internet-facing surface. Two advisories caught in the pre-launch audit; both closed before launch.
  • State Observer. The Git-backed push queue and PR gate. Dev sessions never pushed to GitHub directly; the commit went into a queue that fanned out to GitHub and an internal mirror, auto-opened and gated the PR, and surfaced every repo’s remote state on one dashboard. Verified-pushed or visibly pending. Never silently dropped.
  • Logging Observer. The observability backplane. Loki, Promtail, Prometheus, Grafana. Every service call streamed with PII redaction at ingest. Two hundred and thirty pre-built dashboards for the Observer suite. The record of what happened when nothing was supposed to happen.
Three

One VPS, one container. On purpose.

customOrg is a full e-commerce business running on a single server. One machine hosts everything a customer or operator touches: the storefront, the 3D design editor, the payment flow with Stripe, the Canada Post shipping integration, and the admin console. No separate database server, no managed cloud services, no orchestration layer that needs an on-call rotation. The admin console is reachable only from inside Techmeka’s secure network, so the public internet cannot probe it at all. Nightly backups are automatically restored every Sunday to prove they actually work. A backup you can’t restore isn’t a backup. The result is a system whose surface area is small enough to audit end-to-end at any time, which is the reason customOrg moved from brief to accepting live payments in two months.

Four

If you’re building the next one.

Techmeka runs every production application with the same gates: tasks, tests, security, state, and observability. If your product has any of the pieces customOrg does (a browser surface, a payment flow, operators who need an admin console, an identity you can’t afford to muddle), the workflow already fits. We don’t adapt the workflow to the project; the project moves through the workflow. That’s what makes the cadence repeatable, and that’s what we hire out.
